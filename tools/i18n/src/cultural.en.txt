324	The Umayyad Mosque of Damascus
325	An outstanding architectural masterpiece that joins a long history with the spirit of the old city.
326	The mosque was built under Caliph al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik in the early 8th century AD. It is one of the finest examples of early Islamic architecture, known for its vast courtyard, arcades and historic mosaics.
327	The mosque stands on a sacred site that saw successive forms of worship over thousands of years: the temple of the Aramaean god Hadad, then the Roman temple of Jupiter, then the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist in Byzantine times.
328	In 705 AD Caliph al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik ordered the mosque to be built; the work lasted about ten years, and its walls were decorated with golden mosaics of rivers, trees and palaces that many see as a symbol of paradise.
329	The mosque has three minarets — the Bride's Minaret, the Minaret of Jesus and the Qaitbay Minaret — and its prayer hall holds a shrine attributed to the Prophet John (Yahya). It has suffered fires and earthquakes over the centuries and has been restored many times.

338	Azm Palace in Old Damascus
339	Details of old Damascene life in a courtyard with water at its centre, surrounded by ornament.
340	Built by As'ad Pasha al-Azm in the 18th century, it is one of the finest examples of Damascene architecture from the Ottoman era. Its halls and courtyards reveal traditional furniture, crafts and customs.
341	The palace was built in 1749 by order of As'ad Pasha al-Azm, the Ottoman governor of Damascus, as his residence and seat of government in the heart of the old city near Souq al-Bzouriyeh.
342	The palace has two main parts: the selamlik for receiving guests and the haramlik for the family. Around its courtyards are halls decorated with painted wood (ajami), striped ablaq stone and fountains.
343	The palace was damaged during the Great Syrian Revolt of 1925; it later passed to the state, was restored, and the Museum of Popular Traditions opened there in 1954.

348	The Citadel of Damascus
349	Historic fortress
350	The historic Citadel of Damascus
351	Walls and towers that tell chapters of the city's defence and political importance.
352	The citadel is bound up with the military and political history of Damascus and went through architectural changes in the Middle Ages. Together with the walls of the old city, it is an important part of the historic landscape around the markets and quarters.
353	The citadel stands in the north-western corner of the old city; its site has been fortified since the Seljuk period in the 11th century AD.
354	Al-Adil, the Ayyubid king and brother of Saladin, rebuilt it between 1203 and 1216 with massive towers and strong walls, making it the seat of power and the centre of the city's defence.
355	The citadel was later used as barracks and a prison until the late 20th century, then emptied and restored to become a cultural space hosting festivals and exhibitions.

362	Heritage market
363	Souq al-Hamidiyah in Damascus
364	A bustling commercial passage where popular memory sits alongside crafts and goods.
365	One of the most famous historic markets of Damascus, known for its covered passage and wide variety of shops. It leads towards the Umayyad Mosque as part of the network of connected markets in the old city.
366	The market runs from the Citadel of Damascus to the columns of the Temple of Jupiter in front of the Umayyad Mosque, nearly 600 metres long; it is the most famous and busiest covered market in Damascus.
367	It is named after two Ottoman sultans: Abdul Hamid I, under whom the first part of the market was built around 1780, and Abdul Hamid II, under whom it was extended and rebuilt in the late 19th century.
368	It is known for its arched metal roof, pierced with small holes that let in rays of sunlight, with fabric, sweet and craft shops lining both sides.

374	Khan As'ad Pasha in Old Damascus
375	An architectural space designed to receive merchants and caravans and trade goods.
376	The khan was built under As'ad Pasha al-Azm in 1751 and was part of the city's commercial fabric. Its central courtyard and domes give the place its distinctive architectural presence.
377	As'ad Pasha al-Azm built it between 1751 and 1753 in Souq al-Bzouriyeh as the largest khan in Old Damascus and a centre for caravan trade from many cities.
378	It has two floors around a square courtyard topped by adjoining domes around a central opening, and its walls alternate courses of black and white stone, known as Damascene ablaq.
379	The ground floor was used to store goods and the upper floor to house merchants; it has been restored in recent decades and now hosts exhibitions and cultural events.

383	The National Museum of Damascus

385	Archaeological and cultural museum
386	A journey through Syria's antiquities, from ancient civilisations to Islamic art.
387	The museum holds archaeological collections documenting many sides of Syria's history, introducing visitors to many civilisations and archaeological sites through the objects, inscriptions and works of art on display.
388	Founded in 1919, the museum moved to its current building near the Takiyya Sulaymaniyya in 1936; it is Syria's oldest and largest museum.
389	Its entrance is fronted by the façade of the Umayyad palace of Qasr al-Hayr al-Gharbi from the 8th century AD, moved stone by stone from the Syrian desert and rebuilt here.
390	It has sections for prehistory and for ancient, classical and Islamic Syrian antiquities. Highlights include the Dura-Europos synagogue with its wall paintings and the Ugarit tablet bearing one of the oldest alphabets in the world.

395	The Temple of Jupiter in Damascus
396	Ruins of a Roman temple
397	Towering Roman columns greet visitors leaving Souq al-Hamidiyah for the Umayyad Mosque.
398	The remains of the western gate of the Temple of Jupiter in Damascus, one of the largest temples in Roman Syria, with Corinthian columns and an arch still standing in the heart of the old city.
399	From the first millennium BC a temple to the Aramaean god Hadad stood here; the Romans enlarged it in the 1st and 2nd centuries AD into a temple of Damascene Jupiter.
400	The temple was enclosed by two concentric walls, and the remains of its western gate, with Corinthian columns and an arch, still stand at the end of Souq al-Hamidiyah.
401	In the late 4th century AD the temple became the Church of Saint John the Baptist, and the Umayyad Mosque later rose in its place, so the site sums up thousands of years of worship in Damascus.

409	The gate dates from Roman times and was designed with three openings: a wide central one for carts and two side ones for pedestrians, facing the colonnades that once lined the Straight Street.

446	Damascus's gateway to Medina in the days of the Hejaz Railway.
447	The main passenger station of the Hejaz Railway, with a decorated façade and a hall with a painted wooden ceiling; several old steam locomotives are displayed around it.

488	A square in the heart of modern Damascus with a statue of War Minister Yusuf al-Azma at its centre.
489	One of the best-known squares of modern Damascus, centred on a statue of Yusuf al-Azma, who fell at the Battle of Maysaloun, and overlooked by official and commercial buildings.
490	Yusuf al-Azma, War Minister in the Arab Government in Damascus, led volunteers against the French army at the Battle of Maysaloun on 24 July 1920 and was killed there, becoming a symbol of sacrifice in Syrian memory.
